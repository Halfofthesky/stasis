# Stasis: where the disagreement sits

For every disagreement a text engages, find the level of question it turns on: fact, definition, cause, value or action. Then check that the text meets its opponents at that level, does not skip a level its readers still contest, and says what it is really arguing for. `argument-analyst` asks whether an argument holds; this skill asks whether it is aimed at the right question. The stases tell the writer "where to think," not "what to think" (F&S 429), and the same holds for the reader.

Sources:
- Jeanne Fahnestock and Marie Secor, "The Stases in Scientific and Literary Argument," *Written Communication* 5.4 (1988): 427–443. Cited as F&S, by page.
- Cicero, *On Invention*, Book I, English translation from Wikisource (translator not named in the file; probably the nineteenth-century Bohn translation by C. D. Yonge, unverified). Cited by the chapter numbers of that text, which may not match the Loeb numbering.
- Hermagoras, Quintilian (*Institutio Oratoria* III) and Hermogenes are background only. F&S name them (428); they were not consulted.

## When it applies

The skill needs a disagreement: a claim the text makes against someone, or a claim someone could contest. Opponents can be named ("Smith argues"), grouped ("critics", "the standard view") or implied by the claim ("contrary to what is usually assumed"). Cicero: every case contains one of the issues, and where none applies there is no dispute at all, so no case (ch. 8). If the text reports without contesting anything and makes no contestable claim, say the skill does not apply and stop.

## Modes

1. **Audit**: the user's own draft. Map, diagnose, flag, and propose moves. Do not rewrite the text.
2. **Read**: someone else's text. Map it and say where its disagreements actually sit and whether they meet. No verdict on the work unless asked. If given several texts from one dispute (a literature review, a debate the user is about to enter), map each, then add one clash table across them.

Infer the mode and state the assumption. Infer the audience the text addresses (discipline, subfield, general readers), because whether a lower stasis is settled depends on it. State that assumption too; ask once if it decides the diagnosis. Work on the disagreements the thesis depends on, up to five; if there are more, say which you chose.

## The five stases

The scheme is F&S's five. They separate out cause, which the ancients folded into definition, because "the rise of science has made questions of cause assume special importance" (428). Each stasis has its own question, its own kind of evidence, and "its own peculiar kinds of warrants" (429).

| Stasis | Question | Typical evidence and warrant | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fact | Did it happen? Does it exist? How extensive is it? Will it happen? | records, data, testimony, dating | Cicero: whether Ulysses killed Ajax; Science reports that identify a protein (F&S 432) |
| Definition | Granted the facts, what is it? Which category does it belong in? | criteria, comparison, a definition applied to a case | Cicero: a sacred vessel stolen from a private place, sacrilege or simple theft? (ch. 8); the Yukon tool, Holocene not Pleistocene (F&S 432–433) |
| Cause | What brought it about? What does it lead to? | mechanism, sequence, rivals eliminated | an article that "isolates an active factor in a process" (F&S 433) |
| Value | Granted what it is, is it good or bad, better or worse, central or minor? | criteria of value, comparison, a community's shared values | Cicero: "whether it be just or unjust; whether it be useful or useless" (ch. 9); canonical standing (F&S 436–439) |
| Action | What should be done? | consequences, feasibility, obligations | food stamps: "Should they be continued, expanded, reduced, eliminated?" (F&S 429) |

Classification rules:
- **Predictions are fact.** Cicero puts a dispute about what will happen under fact: whether harm will follow if Carthage is spared (ch. 8). "X will happen" is fact; "we should do X" is action.
- **Processes are fact or definition.** F&S place articles describing how something works "in the stasis of fact or definition" (433). Cause begins when the text claims that one factor produces another.
- **Interpretation of texts is definition.** Literary criticism "support[s] or dispute[s] characterizations (i.e., interpretations of texts)" (F&S 440). A reading of a poem is a definition claim; that the reading makes the poem better is a value claim.
- **Comparison belongs to the stasis of what is compared.** Which date is earlier is fact; which poet is greater is value.

Hand-offs. When the disagreement sits at definition and the concept itself is at issue, pass it to Concept Check. When it sits at cause in a particular case, pass it to Evidence Tests. Opponents are Argument sources in BEAM's terms; a BEAM map shows where they are engaged.

## Jurisdiction: a separate axis

Cicero's fourth issue is not a fifth stasis on F&S's ladder. In the demurrer the dispute is about who should bring the charge, against whom, before whom, under what law and at what time (ch. 11), not about the deed. (In ch. 8 the translation calls it "action", meaning the legal proceeding; this is a reading of the translation, the Latin not checked.) F&S fold questions of procedure into policy (428). This skill keeps jurisdiction apart, because in academic argument it is common and is often answered in the wrong terms:
- the wrong discipline or method: "this is a question for sociologists, not literary critics";
- the wrong evidence base: "you cannot settle this from published sources";
- the wrong standing: "outsiders cannot judge this tradition";
- the wrong frame or period: "the question makes no sense before 1800".

A jurisdiction objection is answered with an argument about forum, method or standing. Answering it with more facts or a better definition misses it (flag S7).

## Core principles

- **The point to decide lies where the two statements meet.** Cicero defines "the question" as "the dispute which arises from the conflict of the two statements" (ch. 13): "You did so and so;"—"I did not do so;" —or, "it was lawful for me to do so." (ch. 8). Each answer moves the case to a different issue. So the stasis of a disagreement is fixed by both sides, not by the text alone. Record the opponent's claim as well as the text's.
- **Each stasis presupposes agreement below it.** It is a dispute about a name "when parties are agreed as to the fact" (Cicero, ch. 8), and a dispute about quality when both what was done and its name are agreed (ch. 9). F&S: "the first, most basic question must be disposed of before more complex ones can be addressed" (428), or at least assumed (429).
- **The hierarchy follows the audience, not logic.** F&S: the order "does not reflect an externally imposed requirement of validity; rather, that order anticipates the inevitable reactions of Western audiences" (431). A lower stasis can be legitimately assumed when the intended audience grants it. Skipping is a fault only when this audience still contests the step. Always say which audience you are judging for.
- **Pitched is not the same as aimed.** "the stasis in which an argument is pitched is not necessarily the stasis in which the arguer hopes to have an effect" (F&S 430). Facts can be argued into place so that the audience draws the value or the action for itself. Record both stases.
- **Every argument has a prior value argument.** "In a sense all arguers must justify the stasis they have chosen to argue in" (F&S 433). The justification can be explicit, or assumed because the audience already grants it. It becomes necessary when the audience does not.
- **Readers draw the implications.** "As readers we push at arguments" (F&S 431), asking what follows for value and action. Field insiders read these subtexts; outsiders may not.
- **Staying in one stasis is normal.** Disciplinary arguments "often stay in one stasis" (F&S 430). Moving through several is also normal: general articles run from fact to action (F&S 429), and Cicero notes that a deliberative case often contains several issues at once (ch. 10). Map the movement. Flag a shift only when it changes what the evidence supports.
- **No scores.** Use *meets / misses / n.a.* and *settled / contested / unknown*, each with a quoted reason.

## Disciplinary norms

Do not flag disciplinary practice that F&S describe as normal:
- **Science**: fact, definition and cause, with the value of the inquiry assumed. A brief opening appeal ("an area of intense research") is a reminder of significance, not a missing argument (F&S 434–435).
- **Literary criticism**: arguments pitched at fact and definition that serve value, which F&S call "fundamentally epideictic" (438), celebrating or questioning a community's shared values. An argument pitched at definition and aimed at value is the genre, not a fault. Ending on held paradox rather than a verdict is also the genre (F&S 439–440).
- **A text that addresses a discipline in ways it does not recognise** "in effect tries to change that discipline or splinters off into a new one" (F&S 440). Such a text owes its audience the prior value argument in full. Its absence is a real flag there (S4).

For other fields, say which norm you assume and where it comes from. That is your own field knowledge: label it so.

## Procedure

1. **Disagreement inventory.** For each disagreement: a short verbatim anchor with location; the opponent (named, grouped, implied); the opponent's claim as the text reports it. If the opponent's own text is available, quote that as well. Where the opponent's claim must be reconstructed, label it reconstructed.
2. **Stasis pair.** Assign a stasis to the text's claim and to the opponent's claim. State the point to decide: the question a neutral reader would have to settle between them. Mark *meets* if both claims sit at the stasis of that question, *misses* if not, and say which side moved.
3. **Profile.** The stasis of the thesis as pitched, and as aimed. The movement of the text by section, written as a line, e.g. `§1 Fact → §2–3 Definition → §4 Value (aimed throughout)`.
4. **Presuppositions.** For the thesis stasis, list the lower stases it presupposes. For each, state the claim it assumes, and whether this audience treats it as *settled*, *contested* or *unknown*, with the evidence: the text's own concession, a named opponent, or your knowledge of the field, labelled.
5. **Justification.** Quote where the text justifies addressing this question in this stasis, or say why the audience can be assumed to grant it, or why it cannot.
6. **Implications.** The value and action implications field readers will draw, and whether the text owns them, disclaims them, or ignores them.
7. **Jurisdiction.** Note any objection about forum, method, evidence base or standing, raised by the text or against it, and how it is answered.
8. **Diagnosis.** Where the dispute really sits, whether the text meets it there, and what it leaves unargued.
9. **Flags**, each with location, and whether it touches the thesis or is local.
10. **Moves** (Audit only). For each flag, name what would fix it: argue the contested lower stasis first; restate the claim at the stasis the opponent occupies; say openly what the argument aims at; add or shorten the prior value argument for this audience; answer the jurisdiction objection as one. Give the cost of each move, such as length, a narrower claim, or a different audience.

## Flags

- **S1 Missed clash.** The text answers an opponent at a different stasis from the opponent's claim, for example meeting a dispute about definition with more facts, or a dispute about value with a causal story. Both sides may be right at their own stasis, and the dispute is left where it was. [Cicero ch. 13]
- **S2 Skipped stasis.** The thesis sits at a higher stasis while a lower one it presupposes is contested for this audience. Examples: a value judgement on a classification still in dispute; a proposal resting on a disputed cause. [Cicero chs. 8–9; F&S 428–429, 431]
- **S3 Hidden aim.** The argument is pitched at one stasis and aimed at another, and the aimed claim carries the thesis but gets no support of its own, or the text disclaims it. Not a flag when the genre makes the aim plain to its readers (see Disciplinary norms). [F&S 430, 436–437]
- **S4 Unjustified stasis.** The text gives no reason to address this question at this stasis, and its audience cannot be assumed to grant one: a new topic, an outside audience, a text that challenges the discipline's frame. [F&S 433–435, 440]
- **S5 Wrong-stasis support.** A claim at one stasis is supported only by material of another, with no warrant bridging them: facts offered as if they settled a value, a classification offered as if it settled a cause. [F&S 429]
- **S6 Unowned implication.** Field readers will draw a value or action implication that the text neither argues nor disclaims, or that it disclaims while its wording argues it (evaluative vocabulary in a "descriptive" account). [F&S 431, 436]
- **S7 Jurisdiction missed.** An objection about forum, method, evidence base or standing is answered as if it were about fact or definition, or it goes unanswered. [Cicero ch. 11]
- **S8 Lumped opponents.** Opponents who disagree with the text at different stases are grouped together ("critics", "the literature") and answered once, so the reply misses most of them.
- **S9 Ambiguous stasis.** The text supports two readings of the stasis of a claim or of an opponent's claim, and nothing in it decides between them. Give both and say what hangs on each: which flags apply under each reading. Do not resolve it by guessing intent.

S1 and S8 apply Cicero's account of the point of dispute to written academic debate. F&S do not discuss opponents talking past each other beyond calling two Science articles "charge and countercharge in the first stasis" (433). Present these two flags as this skill's extension of the doctrine.

## Output

Lead with the diagnosis, then the flags, then the clash table, the profile line and the presupposition table, then the moves (Audit only). No closing summary. For a long text or several texts, put the clash table in a file and give the rest in the reply.

## Don't

- Score texts or arguments.
- Treat the hierarchy as a rule of logic. Judge skipping against the stated audience.
- Flag a genre's normal gap between pitched and aimed stasis as a fault.
- Fix an opponent's stasis from the text's summary alone when the opponent's own words are available. Where they are not, say the stasis rests on the text's report.
- Present your own knowledge of a field's norms or of what its readers contest as fact without marking it.
- Judge whether claims are true. That is a different task; hand off cause to Evidence Tests and contested concepts to Concept Check.
- Rewrite the text. Propose moves; edit only on explicit request.
- Force one stasis onto an ambiguous claim.
